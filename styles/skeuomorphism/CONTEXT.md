# Skeuomorphism

## Definition
Skeuomorphism in digital design is the practice of making interface elements resemble their real-world physical counterparts — leather textures, paper edges, realistic buttons that look pushable, wood grain, stitched details, etc. It was dominant in early smartphone and desktop interfaces (especially Apple pre-iOS 7) as a way to help users transfer physical knowledge to digital tools.

## Core Characteristics
- Realistic textures, materials, and lighting
- Bevels, shadows, and dimensional cues that mimic physical objects
- Interface elements that look like their analog ancestors (calendars, notebooks, cameras, calculators)
- Rich detail and ornamental realism
- Strong affordance signaling through visual resemblance

## Dos
- Use physical metaphor when it genuinely helps users understand function
- Keep lighting, material, and perspective consistent across the interface
- Ensure the metaphor does not become more important than the actual task
- Provide clear feedback that matches the implied physical behavior

## Don’ts
- Decorate for decoration’s sake once users already understand the digital paradigm
- Create false affordances (things that look pressable but aren’t, or vice versa)
- Let heavy textures and realism reduce legibility or performance
- Apply skeuomorphism inconsistently (mixed metaphors confuse)

## Particularities
- Extremely useful in the early years of touch interfaces when users needed training wheels.
- Became a target of criticism for visual clutter and “dishonesty” once digital literacy rose.
- Still appears in games, certain creative tools, and nostalgic or playful interfaces.
- The underlying idea (help users by referencing the known world) remains valid even when the visual style is abandoned.

## How to Achieve
1. Identify the physical object or material that best maps to the digital function.
2. Render lighting, texture, and form with internal consistency.
3. Make interactive elements look and behave as their physical counterparts would.
4. Test whether the metaphor accelerates understanding or merely adds noise.

## When to Use
- Onboarding users who are new to a digital paradigm
- Playful, narrative, or game experiences
- Products that benefit from tactile or material storytelling
- Nostalgic or heritage-oriented interfaces

## When to Avoid
- Modern productivity and utility apps where speed and clarity matter more than metaphor
- Design systems that need to scale across many contexts with minimal visual weight
- Situations where realistic rendering harms performance or accessibility

## Related
- Directly supported Norman’s early thinking about mapping and affordances
- Largely rejected by Flat Design and later minimal digital styles
- Can be selectively revived for specific emotional or educational goals
