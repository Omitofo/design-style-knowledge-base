# Flat Design

## Definition
Flat design is a digital interface style that rejects skeuomorphic realism in favor of two-dimensional shapes, solid colors, simple iconography, and minimal use of shadows, gradients, or textures. It emphasizes clarity, efficiency, and a distinctly digital aesthetic. It became dominant after iOS 7 (2013) and Microsoft’s Metro/Modern design language.

## Core Characteristics
- Two-dimensional shapes and solid fills
- Limited or no drop shadows, bevels, or realistic textures
- Bright or carefully chosen solid color palettes
- Simple, geometric or highly simplified icons
- Strong typography and hierarchy through size/weight/color rather than depth
- Generous use of white space or color fields
- Focus on content and function over material metaphor

## Dos
- Create clear visual hierarchy with type, color, and spacing
- Use color and shape consistently to signal interactivity
- Ensure sufficient contrast for accessibility
- Keep iconography simple and recognizable
- Maintain alignment and spacing systems

## Don’ts
- Remove all depth cues to the point that interactive elements become ambiguous
- Use low-contrast color combinations
- Over-simplify icons until they lose meaning
- Treat “flat” as an excuse for weak hierarchy or poor spacing

## Particularities
- Flat design was partly a reaction to the visual excess of late skeuomorphism and partly a performance/modernity statement.
- Pure flat design sometimes suffered from weak affordance signaling; later styles (Material, subtle shadows, etc.) reintroduced limited depth.
- It pairs naturally with strong typographic systems and modular grids.

## How to Achieve
1. Eliminate realistic textures, heavy gradients, and dimensional chrome.
2. Build hierarchy through scale, weight, color, and position.
3. Use a limited, high-clarity palette.
4. Design icons as simple geometric or glyph forms.
5. Test interactive elements for discoverability without relying on skeuomorphic cues.

## When to Use
- Modern digital products that want a clean, contemporary, content-first feel
- Design systems that need to be lightweight and scalable
- Brands that want to signal digital-native clarity

## When to Avoid
- Experiences that benefit from strong material or tactile storytelling
- Audiences still learning digital conventions (where extra affordance cues help)
- Contexts where pure flatness reduces usability

## Related
- Direct reaction to Skeuomorphism
- Foundation for many subsequent digital styles (Material Design builds on and softens it)
- Compatible with Minimalism and Swiss clarity principles
