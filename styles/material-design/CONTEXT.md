# Material Design

## Definition
Material Design is Google’s design system (introduced 2014, evolved through Material Design 3) that treats the interface as a tactile, paper-like material existing in a three-dimensional space. It combines the clarity of flat design with meaningful depth, responsive motion, and a consistent system of components, elevation, and interaction patterns.

## Core Characteristics
- Paper-like surfaces with realistic (but stylized) elevation and shadow
- Bold, intentional color (including dynamic color in later versions)
- Responsive motion that explains spatial relationships and hierarchy
- Consistent component library and interaction patterns
- Typography and iconography designed as a coherent system
- Adaptive layouts and theming (including dark theme and dynamic color)

## Dos
- Use elevation and shadow purposefully to indicate hierarchy and interactivity
- Make motion informative (not merely decorative)
- Follow the component and spacing systems for consistency
- Leverage color systems that support accessibility and theming
- Design for the full range of devices and input methods

## Don’ts
- Apply random shadows or motion without spatial meaning
- Ignore the system’s accessibility and adaptive guidelines
- Mix Material components with incompatible visual languages without care
- Treat Material as pure visual style rather than a complete interaction system

## Particularities
- Material is both a visual language and a comprehensive design system (components, motion, color, type, accessibility).
- Later versions (Material You / Material Design 3) emphasize personalization, dynamic color, and greater flexibility.
- It sits between pure flat design and more skeuomorphic approaches — depth is present but highly systematized.

## How to Achieve
1. Adopt or adapt the Material color, type, and elevation scales.
2. Use components consistently rather than inventing parallel patterns.
3. Design motion that clarifies cause and effect and spatial relationships.
4. Test across light/dark and dynamic color scenarios.
5. Prioritize accessibility (contrast, touch targets, focus states) as built-in, not optional.

## When to Use
- Android and cross-platform products that benefit from Google’s ecosystem patterns
- Applications that need a complete, battle-tested component and interaction system
- Products that want clear hierarchy through elevation and motion

## When to Avoid
- Brands that need a highly distinctive or anti-Google visual identity
- Extremely minimal or experimental interfaces that reject system constraints
- Contexts where the Material metaphor feels inappropriate

## Related
- Builds on Flat Design while restoring meaningful depth
- Shares system-thinking DNA with Swiss and design-system practice
- Complements Norman’s principles through clear feedback and spatial mapping
