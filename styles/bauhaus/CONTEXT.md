# Bauhaus

## Definition
The Bauhaus (1919–1933) was a German school that unified art, craft, and industrial design. Its core idea: form follows function; design should be honest to materials and production methods; geometry, primary colors, and industrial materials are preferred over historical ornament.

Key figures: Walter Gropius, László Moholy-Nagy, Wassily Kandinsky, Paul Klee, Josef Albers, Marcel Breuer, Marianne Brandt.

## Core Characteristics
- Geometric forms (circle, square, triangle)
- Primary colors + black/white/gray
- Sans-serif or highly geometric type
- Integration of art and technology
- Honesty of materials and construction
- Rejection of historical styles and superfluous decoration
- Emphasis on mass production and social purpose

## Dos
- Let function and material dictate form
- Use geometry and limited color purposefully
- Design for reproducibility and clarity
- Integrate type, image, and structure as a single system

## Don’ts
- Apply Bauhaus as mere "look" without functional thinking
- Add decorative elements that contradict material honesty
- Ignore the social and educational ideals that originally drove the school

## Particularities
- Bauhaus was a school and a movement, not a single style. Its influence runs through Swiss Style, modern product design, and much of 20th-century graphic design.
- Color theory from Albers (Interaction of Color) is a direct Bauhaus legacy.

## How to Achieve
1. Start with function and production constraints.
2. Reduce to geometric primitives and a strict color set.
3. Prefer type that is clear and modern (geometric sans).
4. Make construction and structure visible where appropriate.
5. Test whether every element serves a purpose.

## When to Use
- Educational, cultural, or industrial contexts
- Systems that need strong geometric identity
- Projects that want to signal modernity and rationality

## Related
- Direct ancestor of Swiss/International Style and much of modernist design
- Shares values with Rams (though Rams is later and more product-focused)
