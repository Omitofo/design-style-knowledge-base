# Minimalism — Style Contract

## Definition
Minimalism in design reduces visual elements to the absolute essentials required for communication or function. White space (or negative space) is treated as a primary compositional tool, not empty background. The style prioritizes clarity, hierarchy, and the removal of non-essential decoration.

Historical roots: Bauhaus functionalism → Swiss Style → Dieter Rams → modern digital minimalism (Apple, many SaaS products).

## Core Characteristics
- Extreme reduction of elements
- Generous, deliberate white space
- Limited color palette (often monochrome or 1–2 accent colors)
- Clean, usually sans-serif typography with strong hierarchy
- High contrast for readability
- Alignment and consistent spacing systems
- Focus on content or product rather than ornament

## Dos
- Use white space as structure (it creates hierarchy and breathing room)
- Establish a clear visual hierarchy with type size, weight, and position
- Choose typefaces that remain legible at multiple sizes with minimal styling
- Ensure every element has a clear purpose
- Test reduction: if removing something does not harm function or understanding, remove it

## Don’ts
- Confuse emptiness with intentionality (sparse does not automatically equal good)
- Sacrifice usability or accessibility for visual purity
- Over-rely on fashionably thin type or low-contrast color that harms readability
- Apply minimalism dogmatically to every context (some messages need energy)
- Forget that "as little design as possible" still requires thorough detailing (Rams #8)

## Particularities
- Minimalism is not the absence of design; it is highly disciplined design.
- It performs best when the underlying content or product is strong.
- Cultural note: Western minimalism often differs from Japanese concepts of *ma* (interval/space) and *wabi-sabi*.

## How to Achieve
1. Start with content hierarchy — what must be seen first, second, third?
2. Establish a strict spacing scale (e.g., 4/8/16/32/64).
3. Limit palette to 1–3 colors + neutrals.
4. Choose one primary type family + one accent if needed.
5. Ruthlessly edit decorative elements, shadows, borders, and icons until only the necessary remain.
6. Validate with Rams’s 10th principle and Norman’s visibility/affordance tests.

## When to Use
- Brand systems that need longevity and clarity
- Product interfaces where task completion is primary
- High-end or luxury positioning that signals restraint
- Data-heavy or content-heavy environments that need breathing room

## When to Avoid
- Campaigns that require high emotional energy or cultural signaling of abundance
- Youth-oriented or subcultural contexts that value density and expression
- Situations where the product or content itself is weak (minimalism exposes weakness)

## Related Principles & Styles
- Directly supported by Rams (especially #5, #7, #10) and Swiss clarity
- Opposite pole: Maximalism
- Close relatives: Swiss/International, Luxury Minimal, certain Mid-Century applications

## Representative Examples
- Braun products (Rams era)
- Apple product design and early iOS (post-skeuomorphism shift)
- Muji
- Many contemporary SaaS and fintech interfaces
