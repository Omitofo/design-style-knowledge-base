# Practical Palette Construction & Harmony

## Foundational Approaches

### 1. Limit and Structure
Most durable systems use a small number of intentional colors rather than many ad-hoc ones:
- Neutrals (backgrounds, text, borders)
- Primary brand / action color
- Secondary / supporting colors
- Semantic colors (success, warning, error, info) when needed

### 2. Classic Harmony Structures (use as starting points, not laws)
- **Monochromatic**: Variations in value and saturation of a single hue. Safe, cohesive, can lack energy.
- **Analogous**: Neighboring hues on the color wheel. Harmonious, natural feeling.
- **Complementary**: Opposite hues. High contrast and energy; use carefully so one dominates.
- **Split-complementary / Triadic**: More complex balanced tension.

Albers teaches that these structures are only starting points — actual interaction in layout matters more than wheel theory.

### 3. Value First
Establish a clear value (lightness) structure before finalizing hues. Hierarchy and accessibility depend more on value contrast than on hue contrast.

### 4. Testing in Context
- Always evaluate the palette on real backgrounds, real type, and real components.
- Test light and dark modes separately.
- Check contrast ratios for text and interactive elements (WCAG).

## Practical Rules of Thumb
- One dominant color, one strong accent, neutrals to support.
- Reserve the strongest saturation or contrast for the most important actions or focal points.
- In data visualization, follow Tufte: color should carry information, not decoration.
- Cultural meanings of color are real but not universal — research for the specific audience.
